

# STRAND: ink-optics
VERDICT FOR RISO/CAM: the literature is essentially unanimous that (a) a single fixed Yule-Nielsen n=2 is not defensible, and (b) extrapolating overprints from ink-on-paper measurements via a Beer-Lambert reflectance product is the single largest modelled error in a system like this. Both claims are backed by measured numbers below.

ON n=2. The n-factor is an empirical lumped parameter for lateral light propagation in the substrate plus non-uniform ink thickness — it is NOT a physical constant. Ruckdeschel & Hauser (1978) derived it from the paper PSF and showed n→1 for a specular/non-scattering support and n→2 for a perfect diffuser, but ALSO that n drifts badly in shadows and that empirical fits are only valid below ~50% area coverage. Hébert & Hersch's 2015 review reports fitted optimal n across real print setups ranging from 1.5 to 10 (Table 3): n rises monotonically with halftone screen frequency (n=2 at 50 lpi → n=9 at 125 lpi on the same paper/printer), rises with paper scattering, and rises with ink density. Rossier & Hersch (2010) showed n should differ PER INK when inks have different optical/mechanical properties. Viggiano (2010) showed that when ink PENETRATES the substrate — which is exactly what RISO's oil/soy emulsion inks do on uncoated stock — the best-fit n goes NEGATIVE (measured ≈ -3.8 on fiber inkjet paper, vs +3.8 to +5.8 on non-penetrating media), and that YN with n=-1 is algebraically almost identical to Kubelka-Munk K/S. So for RISO's regime (coarse screen ~ low lpi, very scattering absorbent paper, heavy ink penetration) n=2 is a coincidence at best. Mazauric et al. (2018) give the modern escape hatch: a closed-form "mean-path-defined YN" that computes a per-halftone spectral parameter from calibration reflectances instead of fitting n at all, with accuracy comparable to fitted YN.

ON INK-ON-INK. No serious model in this literature predicts overprints analytically from ink-on-paper alone. Every Neugebauer-family model REQUIRES the 2^k solid colorant patches (including all ink-on-ink solids) as measured input — that is the non-negotiable calibration floor. Beyond that, Hersch & Crété (2005) is the paper that most directly answers RISO/CAM's question: dot gain is materially different when an ink halftone sits on paper vs on one solid ink vs on two, and modelling that with superposition-dependent ink-spreading curves (12 curves for 3 inks; 32 for CMYK, reducible per Bugnon et al. 2008) cut mean ΔE94 by factors of 1.7–3.4 (e.g. inkjet 75 lpi: 3.03 → 0.90). Hébert & Hersch 2015 independently confirm >50% error reduction from superposition-dependent vs basic ink spreading. Separately, Deshpande/Green/Pointer show the naive reflectance-product overprint assumption is systematically biased — it UNDERESTIMATES overprint reflectance (predicts too dark), and their SCOP model corrects it with a per-channel power/scaling law X = j·(X_bg·X_fg)^k where j and k depend on colorant opacity, ink sequence and dot area, fitted from ink step-wedges over white/grey/black backgrounds. That gets ~1.76–2.15 mean CIEDE2000 on offset spot colours and 2.06 for full 7-colour separation. SCOP is the closest thing in the literature to "measure each ink cheaply, still predict overprints" and is probably the highest-value single change for RISO/CAM if measuring all ink-on-ink solids is off the table.

MODEL RANKING FROM THE REVIEW (Hébert & Hersch 2015, Table 3, mean ΔE94): Cellular YNSN < YNSN < Clapper-Yule, consistently. Cellular YNSN wins everywhere but costs 27 primaries for 3 inks / 625 for 4. Clapper-Yule is the only classical model with explicit physics (Fresnel at the print-air interface + multiple internal reflections + Saunderson-like terms), is better at HIGH screen frequency and worse at low, and fails badly (ΔE94 ≈ 3.0) on low-scattering translucent supports. Kubelka-Munk (Kubelka 1948) underlies the substrate/ink-layer optics but is not itself a halftone model; its K/S is the n=-1 limit of YN. Clapper-Yule's roughness extension (Hébert & Hersch 2005) shows surface roughness is negligible for all normal papers — do not bother modelling it.

ON PREUCIL / TRAPPING. Preucil's 1953/1958 trap equation (TRAP = (D_op − D_1)/D_2) is a densitometric QC metric that assumes density additivity and attributes all deviation to a thinner second ink film. It is not a predictive optical model and is known to break where the additivity/proportionality rules fail. Nguyen (2022) shows it correlates linearly with ΔEab of secondaries, so it is usable as a scalar "how much second ink actually transferred" diagnostic to feed a real model — but do not use it AS the overprint model.

- "Review of spectral reflectance models for halftone prints: principles, calibration, and prediction accuracy" Mathieu Hébert, Roger D. Hersch (2015, Color Research & Application, Vol. 40, No. 4, pp. 383–397 (Wiley)) [found-and-read]
  https://doi.org/10.1002/col.21907
  IDEA: Head-to-head review and benchmark of the five models that actually work in practice: spectral Neugebauer, Yule-Nielsen modified spectral Neugebauer (YNSN), cellular YNSN, Clapper-Yule, low-scattering Clapper-Yule, and the two-by-two dot-centering model — all combined with ink-spreading calibration. Benchmarked on 125–729 patch sets across offset, inkjet and electrophotography, 4 papers, 4 screen frequencies (50/75/100/125 lpi), 3 measurement geometries, and 3- and 4-ink sets. Cellular YNSN is consistently most accurate; YNSN next; Clapper-Yule last except at high screen frequency.
  ACTION: Adopt YNSN + superposition-dependent ink spreading as the baseline, with cellular YNSN as the accuracy upgrade if patch budget allows. Concretely: fitted n in their Table 3 spans 1.5–10 and rises with screen frequency (n=2 @50 lpi, 3 @75, 6 @100, 9 @125 on identical paper/ink/printer) — so RISO/CAM must FIT n per paper+screen combination, not hardcode 2. Superposition-dependent ink spreading beats basic ink spreading by >50% error reduction. Calibration cost: 8 solid primaries + 12 halftone patches @50% for 3-ink superposition-dependent YNSN; 27 primaries + 8 halftones for cellular. Clapper-Yule needs no n but needs the geometry constants (rs, ri, Tin, Tout — their Table 1 gives values for di:8°, de:8°, 45°:0° at refractive index 1.5) and degrades badly on low-scattering supports (ΔE94 2.99 on tracing paper).

- "Improving the Yule-Nielsen modified spectral Neugebauer model by dot surface coverages depending on the ink superposition conditions" Roger David Hersch, Frédérique Crété (2005, IS&T/SPIE Electronic Imaging, Color Imaging X: Processing, Hardcopy and Applications, Proc. SPIE Vol. 5667, pp. 434–445) [found-and-read]
  https://infoscience.epfl.ch/server/api/core/bitstreams/543108d0-f028-4da4-896a-bb9d32179d23/content
  IDEA: Dot gain is NOT one curve per ink. An ink halftone spreads differently when printed alone on paper, on top of one solid ink, or on top of two — and differently depending on WHICH solid ink is underneath. They fit a separate nominal→effective coverage curve for every superposition condition (12 curves for 3 inks), then at prediction time blend those curves with weights equal to the surface coverages of the underlying colorants, solved by a short fixed-point iteration.
  ACTION: Replace the single per-ink tone curve with a set of superposition-dependent ink-spreading curves f_{i/J}, and solve effective coverages iteratively (4–5 iterations converge). Measured payoff on 729 CMY patches: offset @100 lpi mean ΔE94 1.54→0.90 (1.7×), offset @150 lpi 1.87→1.00 (1.8×), inkjet @75 lpi 3.03→0.90 (3.4×), inkjet @100 lpi 3.08→0.91 (3.4×). Also states the correct way to pick n: sweep n and choose the value minimising mean ΔE94 between predicted and measured spectra over the patch set — do not assume it.

- "Simplified Ink Spreading Equations for CMYK Halftone Prints" Thomas Bugnon, Mathieu Brichon, Roger David Hersch (2008, IS&T/SPIE Electronic Imaging, Color Imaging XIII: Processing, Hardcopy, and Applications, Proc. SPIE Vol. 6807, 680717) [found-and-read]
  https://infoscience.epfl.ch/server/api/core/bitstreams/d57ca8bc-f24d-4ef5-aa16-61b6d77abc41/content
  IDEA: Full superposition-dependent ink spreading needs 32 curves for CMYK, which is expensive and noise-sensitive. This paper measures the marginal contribution of each individual ink-spreading curve to prediction accuracy, shows most are redundant for CMYK, analyses how measurement noise corrupts curve fitting, and proposes a reduced set of ink-spreading equations that is both cheaper and more noise-resilient.
  ACTION: Do not implement all 2^(k-1)·k superposition curves. Use the paper's relevance ranking to keep only the curves that measurably move ΔE, and adopt its simplified equation set for the rest. Also adopt the parabolic single-point approximation for each curve (fit only q'(0.5) and use f(q) = [2−4q'(0.5)]q² + [4q'(0.5)−1]q) — one measured patch per curve instead of three, which is a big win given RISO's per-patch variance.

- "Ink-dependent n-factors for the Yule-Nielsen modified spectral Neugebauer model" Romain Rossier, Roger D. Hersch (2010, CGIV 2010 / 5th European Conference on Colour in Graphics, Imaging, and Vision (IS&T), pp. 202–207) [found-and-read]
  https://library.imaging.org/admin/apis/public/api/ist/website/downloadArticle/cgiv/5/1/art00033
  IDEA: Standard YNSN assumes ONE n applies to all inks in a halftone — i.e. that all inks scatter and vary in thickness the same way. When inks differ optically or mechanically (diffusing vs non-diffusing, fluorescent vs classical), that assumption breaks. They fit a best n per individual ink, then compute a per-patch effective n as a coverage-weighted (parabolic) average of the ink-specific n values, and adapt the ink-spreading curves accordingly.
  ACTION: Store n per ink rather than globally, and compute a per-patch n as the coverage-weighted average of the participating inks' n values. Their own caveat is important and honest: for classical, similar inks the improvement is very small — so only pay this cost for RISO's genuinely odd inks (fluorescent, metallic, opaque white), where they measured real gains.

- "The Effect of Multiple Internal Reflections on the Densities of Half-tone Prints on Paper" F. R. Clapper, J. A. C. Yule (1953, Journal of the Optical Society of America, Vol. 43, No. 7, pp. 600–603) [found-abstract-only]
  https://doi.org/10.1364/JOSA.43.000600
  IDEA: The founding physical (not empirical) halftone reflectance model. Light entering the print refracts through the ink layer, scatters in the paper bulk, and is then partly trapped by total internal reflection at the print–air interface, bouncing repeatedly and traversing DIFFERENT colorant areas on each pass. Their closed-form equation accounts for these multiple internal reflections and shows earlier treatments understated the effect.
  ACTION: If RISO/CAM wants a model with no fitted exponent, this is it: R = rs + (Tin·Tout·rg·[Σ a_j t_j]²)/(1 − ri·rg·[Σ a_j t_j²]). Note the asymmetry — the numerator has (Σ a_j t_j)² but the denominator has Σ a_j t_j², which is precisely the ink-on-ink coupling term a naive per-ink Beer-Lambert product drops. Ink transmittances are derived from measured solid patches, not measured directly.

- "Yule-Nielsen effect in printing: a physical analysis" F. R. Ruckdeschel, O. G. Hauser (1978, Applied Optics, Vol. 17, No. 21, pp. 3376–3383) [found-abstract-only]
  https://doi.org/10.1364/AO.17.003376
  IDEA: Derives the Yule-Nielsen curve-fitting parameter n from the point spread function describing lateral light scatter in the paper under a halftone pattern. Establishes the physical bracket: n→1.0 as the substrate approaches a specular (non-scattering) surface, n→2.0 as it approaches a perfect diffuser. Also finds n is stable across screen frequency and coverage in the highlights but becomes increasingly erratic in the shadows — a limitation the YN equation cannot express.
  ACTION: Two concrete constraints: (1) treat the n=1..2 range as the pure-optical-scattering interpretation and recognise that any fitted n>2 is absorbing non-optical effects (ink thickness variation, internal reflections) — so don't market n as physical. (2) Do not fit n on shadow patches; their result says empirical n determination is valid only below ~50% area coverage. Fit n on highlight/midtone patches and validate on shadows.

- "Revisited Yule–Nielsen model without fitting of the n parameter" Serge Mazauric, Mathieu Hébert, Thierry Fournel (2018, Journal of the Optical Society of America A, Vol. 35, No. 2, pp. 244–255) [found-abstract-only]
  https://doi.org/10.1364/JOSAA.35.000244
  IDEA: Introduces the mean-path-defined Yule–Nielsen (MPD-YN) model, which eliminates the empirically fitted scalar n entirely. In its place, a spectral parameter is computed in closed form — different for each halftone — from the measured spectral reflectances/transmittances of calibration patches and the Neugebauer primary surface coverages. The derivation comes from flux-transfer theory between print layers, based on the average number of internal reflections light undergoes between two half-layers of the print. Predictive performance on inkjet prints is comparable to classical fitted YN.
  ACTION: Implement MPD-YN as an alternative to fitted-n YNSN: same calibration inputs (solid primary reflectances + Demichel coverages), no n sweep, closed-form spectral parameter per halftone. Accuracy is on par with fitted YN, so the win is not raw ΔE — it's removing a hand-tuned constant that RISO/CAM currently cannot justify per paper/ink/screen.

- "Analysis of the Yule–Nielsen effect with the multiple-path point spread function in a frequency-modulated halftone" Geoffrey Rogers (2018, Journal of the Optical Society of America A, Vol. 35, No. 6, pp. 916–922) [found-abstract-only]
  https://doi.org/10.1364/JOSAA.35.000916
  IDEA: Derives the paper's point spread function from a multiple-path model that treats light transport in turbid media as a random walk, then uses it to obtain a general reflectance expression for frequency-modulated (stochastic) halftones where dot SIZE is fixed and dot COUNT varies randomly. Shows the corresponding line spread function is Lorentzian.
  ACTION: If RISO/CAM simulates at pixel level (which a visual simulator plausibly does), model optical dot gain as an explicit convolution of the ink coverage map with a Lorentzian line/point spread function whose width is a per-paper scattering parameter, rather than as a global exponent. This makes optical dot gain separable from mechanical dot gain — which is the separation the YN n-factor deliberately blurs together.

- "Ink Penetration, Isomorphic Colorant Mixing, and Negative Values of Yule-Nielsen n" J. A. Stephen Viggiano (2010, Proc. IS&T 18th Color and Imaging Conference (CIC18), pp. 285–290) [found-and-read]
  https://library.imaging.org/admin/apis/public/api/ist/website/downloadArticle/cic/18/1/art00050
  IDEA: Explains the long-standing anomaly of NEGATIVE best-fit n values. Shows theoretically that the Yule-Nielsen formula at n = −1 is nearly identical to the Kubelka-Munk K/S ratio used in colorant formulation, and argues that ink PENETRATION into the substrate — as opposed to lateral light spreading — is a distinct physical cause of negative n. Empirically, coarse halftones printed on fiber inkjet paper with dye inks fit n ≈ −3.8, versus +3.8 to +5.8 on media that do not permit penetration. Also recommends parameterising by u = 1/n, which is continuous where n is not.
  ACTION: (1) Parameterise the model by u = 1/n, not n — u is continuous through the singularity, so an optimiser can cross from positive to negative n smoothly. (2) Remove any n ≥ 1 clamp; allow the fit to go negative. (3) Expect and accept a negative n on absorbent RISO stock, and interpret it as ink penetration, not as a fitting bug. (4) Recognise n = −1 ≈ Kubelka-Munk K/S, so a KM-style mixing model is the natural limiting case for the penetrating-ink regime.

- "N-Ink Printer Characterization With Barycentric Subdivision" Vahid Babaei, Roger D. Hersch (2016, IEEE Transactions on Image Processing, Vol. 25, No. 7, pp. 3023–3031) [found-and-read]
  https://doi.org/10.1109/TIP.2016.2560526
  IDEA: Extends cellular YNSN to arbitrary ink counts for JUXTAPOSED (dot-off-dot, non-overlapping) halftones. Because juxtaposed colorants' area coverages form a simplex rather than a cube, the classical cubic cellular subdivision doesn't apply; they use barycentric subdivision of simplexes instead. With N juxtaposed inks and only 2^N − 1 calibration samples, accuracy beats nominal YNSN, and the method sidesteps the total-ink-limit and moiré problems entirely because inks are never superposed.
  ACTION: For RISO's non-superposable inks (metallic, fluorescent, opaque white), model them as juxtaposed colorants under barycentric-subdivision cellular YNSN rather than trying to predict them as overprints at all. Calibration cost is 2^N − 1 samples for N inks. Also confirms the general architecture RISO/CAM should follow: YNSN inside each cell, cellular subdivision outside.

- "Characterisation of the n-colour printing process using the spot colour overprint model" Kiran Deshpande, Phil Green, Michael R. Pointer (2014, Optics Express, Vol. 22, No. 26, pp. 31786–31800) [found-abstract-only]
  https://doi.org/10.1364/OE.22.031786
  IDEA: The spot colour overprint (SCOP) model. Starts from the observation that the overprint of two inks is approximately the PRODUCT of their independently-measured reflectances — but that this product is systematically a linear UNDERESTIMATE of the true reflectance (i.e. predicts too dark). It corrects the product with a fitted scaling factor and exponent per channel: X = j_x·(X_bg·X_fg)^k_x (and likewise Y, Z), where j and k depend on colorant opacity, ink laydown sequence and dot area. Each ink is characterised individually from step-wedges over white/grey/black backgrounds; the model then predicts solids AND halftones of arbitrary overprints, and is inverted via LUTs for separation. Demonstrated on 7-colour offset with mean CIEDE2000 of 2.06 against target spot colours.
  ACTION: Replace the raw Beer-Lambert overprint product with the SCOP correction: predicted = j·(background × foreground)^k per channel, with j and k regressed against dot area and ink sequence. Calibrate from ink step-wedges (0–100% in ~5 steps) printed over three backgrounds — white, grey, black — per ink; that is a small, RISO-feasible chart. Reported accuracy: power regression gives mean CIEDE2000 1.76–2.15 across three substrates, vs 2.89–2.92 for the plain linear (uncorrected-product) model — i.e. the correction roughly halves the error. Full 7-colour reproduction lands at mean CIEDE2000 2.06.

- "New Contributions to the Optics of Intensely Light-Scattering Materials. Part I" Paul Kubelka (1948, Journal of the Optical Society of America, Vol. 38, No. 5, pp. 448–457) [found-abstract-only]
  https://doi.org/10.1364/JOSA.38.000448
  IDEA: The canonical statement of the Kubelka-Munk two-flux theory: an exact differential-equation system for light propagation in a scattering, absorbing layer, with angular distribution captured by parameters u and v. Gives integrated hyperbolic-function solutions for reflectance and transmittance as a function of layer thickness, plus approximations for light, dark, thin and thick specimens, and identifies the conditions under which the familiar simple KM formulas are exact (perfectly diffuse illumination, or parallel light at 60° from normal).
  ACTION: Use KM (not Beer-Lambert) for any ink that SCATTERS rather than merely absorbs — RISO's opaque white, metallic and pearlescent inks. Beer-Lambert with a single transmittance per ink is only valid for transparent, non-scattering films over a diffusing support; a scattering ink needs both K and S. Also note the geometry caveat: the simple KM formulas are exact only for perfectly diffuse illumination or 60° parallel incidence, so the measurement geometry used to derive K and S must match the geometry the simulator claims to reproduce.

- "Extending the Clapper–Yule model to rough printing supports" Mathieu Hébert, Roger David Hersch (2005, Journal of the Optical Society of America A, Vol. 22, No. 9, pp. 1952–1967) [found-abstract-only]
  https://doi.org/10.1364/JOSAA.22.001952
  IDEA: Generalises Clapper-Yule, which assumes a planar print–air interface, by modelling the surface as randomly oriented microfacets and integrating Fresnel reflection with shadowing over incident angles and facet orientations. The key experimental finding is negative: for essentially all commercial papers — coated, calendered, newsprint, and plain copy paper — surface roughness has negligible effect on predicted reflectance. Roughness only matters for very rough, thick, non-diffusing coatings.
  ACTION: Do not build microfacet/roughness modelling into the print–air interface. Keep the planar Clapper-Yule Fresnel constants (rs, ri, Tin, Tout at n=1.5) even for rough uncoated RISO stock. Spend the effort on ink superposition and the n-factor instead, which the measurements show dominate.

- "Study of correlation between ink trapping and color difference in overprint" Nguyen Thanh Phuong (2022, VNUHCM Journal of Engineering and Technology, Vol. 5, No. 3, pp. 1596–1604) [found-abstract-only]
  https://doi.org/10.32508/stdjet.v5i3.994
  IDEA: Reviews the three density-based ink trapping formulas in industrial use — Preucil, Ritz and Brunner — all of which compute apparent trap from solid density values of the primaries and the overprint, and all of which assume density additivity with any deviation attributed solely to a thinner second ink film. Argues density measurement is the wrong instrument for assessing overlap, and shows empirically that ΔEab* of the secondary has a LINEAR correlation with the traditional density-based trap value, proposing colour difference as the more reliable trap indicator.
  ACTION: Do NOT use Preucil TRAP = (D_overprint − D_first)/D_second as RISO/CAM's overprint predictor. Instead, if RISO/CAM wants a physically-motivated 'second ink lays down less on ink than on paper' term, use trap as a per-ink-pair scalar that scales the effective ink amount (or effective coverage) of the second ink before it enters the YNSN/SCOP model — and calibrate that scalar colorimetrically (ΔE-based) rather than densitometrically, per this paper's finding.

# STRAND: fluorescent
Fourteen verified papers, clustering into four strands.

**(1) Why a Beer–Lambert / multiplicative-transmittance model structurally cannot handle fluorescent inks.** The single most useful result is Emmel & Hersch (CIC 1998), who state outright that their fluorescence-aware model beats Beer's law by an average of ~ΔE 17 on real fluorescent-ink-on-paper samples. The physics behind that number is consistent across the literature: (a) fluorescent emission is *additive*, not multiplicative — Hersch's models all take the form `R_total(λ) = R_pure(λ) + E_fluo(λ)`, where R_pure comes from a classical (Clapper–Yule / Yule–Nielsen–Neugebauer) model and E_fluo is added on top; (b) the total radiance factor of a daylight-fluorescent ink can exceed 1.0, which no product-of-transmittances model can produce; (c) fluorescent "reflectance" is *illuminant-dependent* — not a material constant, so a single measured spectrum is only valid under one illuminant (Turunen et al., CGIV 2010; ASTM E2152/E2153 background); (d) layer order matters, because an ink on top of a fluorescent ink attenuates both the incident excitation light *and* the emerging emission, in two different wavelength bands (Hersch 2008, 2014) — whereas a Beer–Lambert product is order-symmetric; (e) at high fluorophore concentration, quenching makes emission *fall* with more ink (Hersch/Donzé/Chosson, SIGGRAPH 2007), the opposite sign of Beer–Lambert's monotone darkening. All five are concrete, testable failure predictions for RISO/CAM's Fl. Pink / Fl. Yellow overprints.

**(2) Bispectral / Donaldson-matrix ground truth and how to get it cheaply.** The gold standard is a two-monochromator bispectrometer producing the full Donaldson matrix (illuminant-independent, excitation × emission). Nobody sane owns one. The important line of work for RISO/CAM is Tominaga, Hirai & Horiuchi, who show the Donaldson matrix is effectively low-rank — it decomposes into three 1-D spectral functions (reflection, excitation, emission) — and can be recovered from as few as **two ordinary illuminants** (JOSA A 2015), or from a spectral-imaging camera under a handful of ordinary broadband sources (Opt. Express 2018; generalized version in IS&T Electronic Imaging 2018, DOI 10.2352/ISSN.2470-1173.2018.8.MAAP-168). Fu et al. (TPAMI 2016) and Blasinski, Farrell & Wandell (IEEE TIP 2020) do the same from the computer-vision side with cameras plus filtered/LED illumination.

**(3) Feasible WITHOUT a spectrophotometer — the practical answer.** Three tiers, cheapest first. (i) **Two-illuminant camera capture**, the Tominaga 2015 idea reduced to buyable hardware: photograph a printed patch chart under (a) a UV/violet ~395–405 nm LED and (b) a warm/neutral broadband LED, raw, fixed exposure, with a white reference. Because fluorescence is excited in one band and emitted in another, two illuminants with very different UV/blue content suffice to separate the additive emission term from the multiplicative reflection term — exactly the separation Beer–Lambert is missing. (ii) **The UVI/UVX trick from Calabria & Rich (CIC 2003)**: shoot the same chart with and without a UV-cut filter over the light; the difference image *is* the fluorescent contribution, and they show it is worth >3 ΔE*ab on brightened stock even for ordinary CMYK. One cheap filter, no spectral instrument. (iii) **Pure empirical / no optics at all**: Deshpande & Green (CIC 2010) predict spot-colour overprints from per-ink solid + tint patches on three backings plus a linear opacity mix, reaching mean CIEDE2000 < 3 with a very small measurement set — this scales to RISO's 28 inks without ever building a bispectral model, and is the realistic first upgrade for RISO/CAM. What is *not* feasible without instruments: absolute total-radiance-factor calibration, quantum yields, and true illuminant-independent Donaldson matrices.

**(4) Multi-ink, spot-colour and opaque/metallic ink modelling.** 28 inks means 2^N Neugebauer primaries is hopeless; Babaei & Hersch's barycentric-subdivision N-ink characterization (IEEE TIP 2016) is the standard tractable formulation. Babaei & Hersch (JIST 2016) is directly relevant to RISO's opaque, high-pigment-load inks: metallic/opaque particles *hide* what is underneath, invalidating the transparent-layer assumption entirely and forcing juxtaposed (side-by-side, non-overlapping) halftoning plus Demichel/Kueppers-style N-colour separation rather than a layer-stack model. RISO overprints are physically closer to this regime than to transparent offset inks.

**Concrete recommendation for RISO/CAM:** keep Beer–Lambert for the 17 non-fluorescent inks, but replace the fluorescent path with the Hersch decomposition `R_total = R_multiplicative + E_additive`, where E_additive per fluorescent ink is (i) attenuated by any ink printed *above* it in the emission band, (ii) attenuated by any ink above it in the excitation band, and (iii) rolled off with coverage to model quenching. Calibrate E_additive from two-illuminant camera captures of overprint patches. Add a per-ink opacity parameter (Deshpande) and Demichel area-coverage algebra (Babaei) before touching fluorescence — those are cheaper wins. Expect the largest errors on Fl.Pink-under-dark-ink and Fl.Yellow + Fl.Pink combinations, and expect asymmetry between the two print orders, which the current model cannot represent at all.

# STRAND: chart-design
Nineteen verified entries (fifteen papers, four standards); every URL was actually fetched, and where a publisher paywalled the page I resolved metadata through PubMed or the OpenAlex API rather than guessing. Wiley (Alsam & Finlayson), ACM DL, ScienceDirect and iso.org all refused fetches, so those are marked found-abstract-only; nothing here is reconstructed from memory.

The literature splits into five strands, and they map cleanly onto the feature.

SELECTION ALGORITHMS. Three families, in increasing sophistication. (1) Space-filling / geometric: Kennard-Stone (1969) is the sequential max-min algorithm the colour field actually uses, and Cheung & Westland's MAXMINC (2006) proved a custom 24-patch set beats the ColorChecker at equal count. (2) Model-aware / D-optimal: Alsam & Finlayson (2008) tie subset selection to the cross-product matrix of colour signals — the colour-science spelling of maximising det(X'X) — and get 24 Macbeth patches down to 13. This is the only genuine D-optimal-flavoured result I could verify in the colour-target literature; explicit "D-optimal design" language is essentially absent, the field reaches the same place via information/Gram-matrix arguments. (3) Interpolation-aware: Morovič et al. (2010, greedy) and Bianco & Schettini (2012, direct search) optimise the sample set against the tessellation-and-interpolation that will consume it. Bianco & Schettini give the headline number for "how few patches" — about one quarter of a uniform grid at matched accuracy, versus one third for the best prior method.

WHERE TO SPEND PATCHES. Chang, Allebach & Bouman (1997) derive, via a vector-quantisation-style asymptotic analysis, that grid density should follow local nonlinearity — applied to colour printer characterisation specifically. Independently, IT8.7/5 / ISO 12642-3 shows the industry's revealed preference under a hard budget: it deleted 29 duplicate patches from IT8.7/4 and spent all 29 on new greys. Both point the same way for a risograph, whose tone curve is savagely nonlinear and whose neutrals are the fragile, built-from-spot-inks part.

CHART LAYOUT AND RANDOMISATION. ISO 12642-2:2006 is the primary source and it is unusually explicit: it defines only *data values*, deliberately avoiding the words "patch" and "target", leaves layout to the user, and supplies two default layouts — one for visual inspection and one randomised "with the goal of uniform ink loading in each colour across the target area to minimize interaction between patch areas". That is the citable justification for randomising, in the standard's own words. ECI2002 does the same and adds a second principle worth stealing: strict nesting, since its 1485 patches are a superset of IT8.7/3's 928, so one print serves both. Architecturally: separate the patch-set generator from the layout engine.

READBACK FROM A PHOTOGRAPH. This is where RISO/CAM diverges from all the print literature, which assumes a spectrophotometer. Ernst et al. (2013) give the engineering recipe — ship the chart as a polygon-plus-reference-colour model, recover pose by homography, score the alignment with a colour-statistics cost. Bala, Sharma & Venable (2003) show that misorientation and row-shift errors should be defended against *in the chart design* via patch codes and pattern matching, not patched downstream; Wu & Dalal (2011) then quantify how many code levels a noisy device can actually carry, and show levels should be spaced in CIELAB, not device space. Karaimer & Nguyen (2020) are the outlier worth taking seriously: they abandon correspondences entirely, matching 3D colour histograms instead, which both removes the four-corner-click failure mode and lets the chart carry thousands of colours.

DEVICE STRUCTURE AND ERROR METRICS. Deshpande, Green & Pointer (2014) matter more than their citation count suggests, because a risograph is an n-ink spot-colour device with user-swapped drums, not a CMYK process printer — so IT8.7/x data sets are not directly usable. Their SCOP model characterises each ink separately over three backings, making chart size linear rather than combinatorial in the number of drums, at ~2.06 ΔE00 on 7-colour offset. That is the structural template I would build the RISO chart on. Eckhard et al. (2014) supply the trick that makes small charts possible without a small search space: synthesise the candidate pool from an ink model, optimise over millions of virtual patches, print only the survivors. For scoring, Sharma (2006) is the citable protocol — A2B/B2A/round-trip ΔE, with the warnings that mean ΔE alone is inadequate (report max and a histogram, and split ΔL*/Δa*/Δb*) and that ΔE across a gamut-mapping stage is meaningless. Held-out evaluation is universal practice in these papers but, notably, none of them formalises a cross-validation protocol for print targets — that appears to be a genuine gap rather than something I failed to find.

Two caveats. First, ISO 13655:2017 imposes a hard physical constraint that is easy to miss: aperture must scale with patch size and screen ruling, and RISO screens are coarse, so patches must be *large*. That caps patches-per-sheet and is the strongest practical argument for why minimal patch sets matter for this device specifically. It also flags backing material and optical-brightener fluorescence, both acute on thin uncoated risograph stock. Second, I could not verify one thing worth chasing: ISO/TS 18621-21 (press-sheet macro uniformity, the "M-Score") appeared only in search snippets and iso.org refused the fetch, so I omitted it as an entry — but a metric for 1D spatial nonuniformity across a sheet is directly relevant to RISO drum banding and would be worth confirming before relying on it. I also found no published work on camera-photographed printer characterisation charts as such; that combination looks genuinely unstudied, so RISO/CAM will be assembling it from the print-side and camera-side literatures rather than following an existing recipe.

- "Computer Aided Design of Experiments" R. W. Kennard, L. A. Stone (1969, Technometrics 11(1), 137–148 (Taylor & Francis / ASA)) [found-abstract-only]
  https://doi.org/10.1080/00401706.1969.10490666
  IDEA: Introduces the CADEX / "Kennard-Stone" sequential space-filling design. Start from the two most-separated candidate points, then repeatedly add the candidate whose distance to its nearest already-selected point is maximal. Each prefix of the resulting ordering covers the experimental region uniformly and includes the boundary of the data cloud.
  ACTION: Implement Kennard-Stone over a dense candidate pool of RISO ink-duty combinations, in a perceptual space, and emit the selection order. Then a 60-patch chart is just the first 60 entries and a 300-patch chart the first 300, with the 60 nested inside the 300 — so a user who prints the big chart later reuses the small chart's data. Kennard-Stone's boundary-inclusion property automatically pins paper white, each solid, and the full overprint, which are the anchors a RISO fit most needs.

- "Methods for Optimal Color Selection" Vien Cheung, Stephen Westland (2006, Journal of Imaging Science and Technology 50(5), 481–488 (IS&T)) [found-abstract-only]
  https://doi.org/10.2352/J.ImagingSci.Technol.(2006)50:5(481)
  IDEA: Studies how to pick an optimal subset of colour samples (from 1269 Munsell surfaces) for characterising a digital camera, and compares the resulting custom charts against the GretagMacbeth ColorChecker. Shows that a 24-sample set chosen by a greedy max-min-chroma-style algorithm they call MAXMINC outperforms the 24-patch ColorChecker, and that the same approach generalises to selecting samples within a constrained colour space.
  ACTION: Use it as the justification for generating rather than shipping a fixed chart, and as a baseline: benchmark RISO/CAM's selected N patches against an evenly-spaced N-patch grid on held-out data. If the custom set does not win, the selector is broken. Note the constrained-space framing: the RISO gamut is small and oddly shaped, which is precisely the regime where custom selection pays off most.

- "Integer programming for optimal reduction of calibration targets" Ali Alsam, Graham D. Finlayson (2008, Color Research & Application 33(3), 212–220 (Wiley)) [found-abstract-only]
  https://doi.org/10.1002/col.20402
  IDEA: Asks how many surfaces in a calibration target are actually needed to reproduce the performance of the whole target. Unlike prior work that reduced charts independently of the downstream task, they tie the reduction to calibration performance: the outcome of colorimetric and spectral calibration depends on the cross-product matrix of the colour signals, so they pose subset selection as a quadratic minimisation with integer constraints. Result: about 13 surfaces emulate the properties of the 24-patch Macbeth ColorChecker.
  ACTION: Once RISO/CAM commits to a regression basis (e.g. a polynomial or root-polynomial in ink duties, or a Yule-Nielsen/Neugebauer parameterisation), select patches by maximising det(X'X) over that basis rather than by geometry alone. Model-aware selection is measurably better than model-blind space filling, and it tells the user honestly how few patches their chosen correction model can survive on.

- "On the Selection of Patches for Color Camera Calibration" Margarita Khokhlova, Jon Yngve Hardeberg (2016, IS&T International Symposium on Electronic Imaging 2016, Color Imaging XXI: Displaying, Processing, Hardcopy, and Applications, COLOR-315) [found-and-read]
  https://library.imaging.org/admin/apis/public/api/ist/website/downloadArticle/ei/28/20/art00013
  IDEA: Runs Kennard-Stone patch selection across sRGB, CIEXYZ, CIELAB and CIELUV, with Euclidean, CityBlock and CIEDE2000 distances, on three reflectance datasets (1269 Munsell, 4826 textiles, 3818 printed samples), then fits linear and 2nd/3rd-order polynomial camera corrections and reports mean and max ΔE*ab. Finds the choice of colour space for *selection* materially changes the final target and the calibration error, that Euclidean distance beat CIEDE2000 for the selection step across all three datasets, and that custom 24-patch targets beat the Macbeth ColorChecker on two of three datasets (with a very large improvement on the two non-Munsell sets).
  ACTION: Do the selection in whatever space the correction is fit in, not reflexively in CIELAB, and make the selection space a tunable you can A/B. Use plain Euclidean distance for the greedy step (cheaper and, per this paper, better). Also reuse their evaluation shape verbatim: report mean AND max ΔE, because a chart can look fine on average while leaving one region of the RISO gamut badly unmodelled.

- "Sampling optimization for printer characterization by greedy search" Ján Morovič, Jordi Arnabat, Yvan Richard, Ángel Albarrán (2010, IEEE Transactions on Image Processing 19(10), 2705–2711) [found-abstract-only]
  https://doi.org/10.1109/TIP.2010.2050111
  IDEA: Uniform sampling of a printer's colorant space is inefficient because the input→colour relationship is strongly nonlinear. Prior reduction methods either fail to exploit the sampling space or bake in assumptions about the underlying relationship. This work greedily searches for sample positions making no assumptions beyond those already implicit in the tessellation-and-interpolation that will consume the samples, and reports a significant reduction in the number of samples needed to hit a given colour accuracy. The optimisation is expensive but happens once, at engineering time, invisibly to the end user.
  ACTION: Adopt the key principle: the sampling criterion must match the interpolator that will use the samples. If RISO/CAM corrects via tetrahedral/scattered interpolation, optimise sample placement for interpolation error, not for geometric uniformity. And do the expensive search offline per ink-set, shipping a static ordering.

- "Sampling optimization for printer characterization by direct search" Simone Bianco, Raimondo Schettini (2012, IEEE Transactions on Image Processing 21(12), 4868–4873) [found-abstract-only]
  https://doi.org/10.1109/TIP.2012.2211029
  IDEA: Direct-search optimisation of the characterisation sample set. Matches a given colour accuracy using on average roughly one quarter of the samples required by uniform sampling, versus about one third for the best prior method (the greedy search above). Sample count drops further when combined with a sequential optimisation stage that refines sample values in the device-independent space, and the method extends to optimising one sample set jointly across multiple substrates with statistically better accuracy than per-substrate optimisation.
  ACTION: Two concrete features. (1) Set the honest expectation in the UI that a well-chosen chart needs roughly a quarter of a grid's patches — that is the size of the win, and it is what makes a one-sheet RISO chart viable. (2) Ship one chart that was optimised jointly over several papers rather than a chart per paper, and cite this as the reason.

- "Sequential Linear Interpolation of Multidimensional Functions" James Z. Chang, Jan P. Allebach, Charles A. Bouman (1997, IEEE Transactions on Image Processing 6(9), 1231–1245) [found-abstract-only]
  https://doi.org/10.1109/83.623187
  IDEA: Introduces sequential linear interpolation (SLI), a partially separable grid that allocates more grid points where the function being interpolated is more nonlinear, cutting interpolation MSE while keeping most of the speed of a uniform grid. Optimal grid-point placement is derived by an asymptotic analysis analogous to vector-quantisation theory, yielding closed-form MSE expressions used to design the grid, plus a post-processing fix for when the asymptotic assumptions fail. Demonstrated on colour printer characterisation.
  ACTION: Make patch density a function of local curvature of the RISO tone/overprint response rather than uniform in ink duty. Practically: ship a coarse pilot ramp, fit a rough response, then let SLI-style density allocation place the main chart's levels — which also gives a principled defence of a non-uniform ink ladder to any user who asks why the steps are unevenly spaced.

- "Adaptive global training set selection for spectral estimation of printed inks using reflectance modeling" Timo Eckhard, Eva M. Valero, Javier Hernández-Andrés, Markus Schnitzlein (2014, Applied Optics 53(4), 709–719 (Optica)) [found-abstract-only]
  https://doi.org/10.1364/AO.53.000709
  IDEA: Rather than measuring a training set, they *model* a large population of realistic printable ink reflectances, then apply a recursive top-down algorithm that rejects clusters of training samples which do not improve linear least-squares regression performance. The scheme beats existing training-selection methods on multispectral camera data, handles very large candidate sets efficiently, and establishes reflectance modelling as a practical way to synthesise training data for print inspection.
  ACTION: Generate the candidate pool from RISO/CAM's own forward ink/overprint simulation, run cluster-wise recursive rejection against the actual fitting objective, and print only the survivors. This is the mechanism that lets the chart be small without the search space being small.

- "Characterisation of the n-colour printing process using the spot colour overprint model" Kiran Deshpande, Phil Green, Michael R. Pointer (2014, Optics Express 22(26), 31786–31800 (Optica)) [found-abstract-only]
  https://doi.org/10.1364/OE.22.031786
  IDEA: Extends the spot colour overprint (SCOP) model into a general forward characterisation model for n-colour printing, and builds an inverse model via lookup tables for separation. Each ink is characterised separately by printing a solid and its tints over three backings — bare substrate, grey, and solid black. Validated by reproducing spot colours on a 7-colour lithographic offset process, achieving mean CIEDE2000 of 2.06 between target and printed colour.
  ACTION: Structure the RISO/CAM chart as SCOP does: per-drum tint ladders printed over a small set of backings (bare paper, plus the other drums' solids/tints), rather than a full factorial of ink combinations. For a 2–3 drum RISO job this collapses the chart to something that genuinely fits on one sheet, and ~2 ΔE00 on a 7-ink press is a realistic accuracy bar to quote.

- "Methodology for Evaluating the Quality of ICC Profiles — Scanner, Monitor, and Printer" Abhay Sharma (2006, Journal of Imaging Science and Technology 50(5), 469–480 (IS&T)) [found-and-read]
  https://doi.org/10.2352/J.ImagingSci.Technol.(2006)50:5(469)
  IDEA: Defines a reusable ΔE merit-figure methodology for grading device profiles. For printers it specifies three metrics — accuracy of the B2A1 tag (PCS→device), the A2B1 tag (device→PCS), and a round-trip test — and reports measured averages of ΔE 1.72–3.49 for an inkjet across commercial profiling packages. Argues profiling tools should always emit a quality number, and warns that a single mean ΔE is inadequate: separate ΔL*, Δa*, Δb* is more diagnostic of casts, and second-order statistics (histograms, cumulative frequency) beat mean-and-max for judging overall match. Also flags that gamut mapping makes ΔE meaningless for perceptual intents.
  ACTION: Report a per-fit quality figure with mean, max, and a ΔE histogram over *held-out* patches, plus separate ΔL*/Δa*/Δb* so a paper-tint cast is distinguishable from a tone-curve error. Score only the colorimetric path; do not compute ΔE across a gamut-mapping stage. Setting a go/no-go threshold gives the 'reprint your chart, the photo was bad' check the feature needs.

- "Adaptive Statistical Methods for Optimal Color Selection and Spectral Characterization of Color Scanners and Cameras" Zsolt Tibor Kosztyán, János Schanda (2009, Journal of Imaging Science and Technology 53(1), 010501-1–010501-10 (IS&T)) [found-abstract-only]
  https://doi.org/10.2352/J.ImagingSci.Technol.(2009)53:1(010501)
  IDEA: A self-adaptive statistical method for choosing optimal representative colour samples so that a detector's spectral responsivity can be estimated more precisely. Characterisation accuracy improves markedly with proper sample selection, and improves further when side information — such as the spectral power distribution of the illuminant — is available to the selection procedure.
  ACTION: Split the chart into two functional groups: patches that characterise the *printer* and patches that characterise the *capture* (a neutral ladder plus known-chromaticity references for white balance and illuminant estimation). Optionally re-plan a second-sheet chart adaptively once the first capture has revealed the lighting.

- "A Correspondence-Free Color Chart Design for Color Calibration" Hakkı Can Karaimer, Rang Nguyen (2020, London Imaging Meeting (LIM'20), IS&T) [found-and-read]
  https://doi.org/10.2352/issn.2694-118X.2020.LIM-01
  IDEA: Argues that colour chart design has not changed in 40+ years and has two flaws: too few colours (the ColorChecker has 18 chromatic + 6 neutral patches), and the need to localise patches in the camera image, usually by having the user click four corners — error-prone under rotation or partial occlusion. They instead print a chart containing *thousands* of colours and estimate the 3x3 transform by aligning the captured image's 3D colour histogram to the chart's known histogram, via a multigrid optimisation, achieving accuracy on par with patch-based calibration with no correspondences at all.
  ACTION: Treat correspondence-free histogram alignment as the fallback path when fiducial detection fails or the sheet is partly occluded, and as a sanity check on the patch-based fit (large disagreement between the two = bad capture). Its multigrid histogram-alignment objective is also the natural way to exploit the RISO's dense, continuous smear of achievable colours rather than only discrete patch centres.

- "Check my chart: A robust color chart tracker for colorimetric camera calibration" Andreas Ernst, Anton Papst, Tobias Ruf, Jens-Uwe Garbas (2013, MIRAGE 2013 — 6th Int. Conf. on Computer Vision / Computer Graphics Collaboration Techniques and Applications (ACM), Berlin) [found-abstract-only]
  https://doi.org/10.1145/2466715.2466717
  IDEA: An algorithm that detects and tracks a colour calibration chart in images robustly against noise and blur. The chart is described by an explicit model — polygons giving the geometry of each region together with the associated reference colours — which is aligned to the observed chart by a homography. A cost function scores alignment quality using colour statistics of the regions, and integral images make per-region colour extraction cheap enough to run in real time. Demonstrated with the X-Rite ColorChecker Classic.
  ACTION: Emit the chart as a model file (patch polygons in chart coordinates + intended ink values + expected colours) alongside the printable PDF, and recover the sheet pose by fitting a homography and scoring it with a colour-statistics cost. Sample each patch's interior with an inset margin so RISO misregistration and edge bleed do not contaminate readings — and use the alignment cost as an automatic 'retake the photo' trigger.

- "Robust Processing of Color Target Measurements for Device Characterization" Raja Bala, Gaurav Sharma, Dennis L. Venable (2003, IS&T PICS Conference (Image Processing, Image Quality, Image Capture Systems)) [found-abstract-only]
  https://api.openalex.org/works/W1596640862
  IDEA: Addresses errors that arise when characterisation targets are measured, specifically misorientation of the target on the measurement stage. Presents two remedies: encoding orientation information into the printed target via patch codes so it can be recovered from the measurements themselves, and pattern matching against the expected colour sequence to detect and automatically correct misalignment, with optional user validation. Both fix orientation errors with minimal operator burden.
  ACTION: Bake orientation and row/column identity into the chart itself — asymmetric fiducials so 180° flips are unambiguous, plus per-row marker sequences that let you verify indexing. Then validate the decoded patch grid by pattern-matching observed values against expected ones before fitting, and reject or auto-correct rather than silently fitting a shifted grid (which would produce a confidently wrong correction).

- "Optimal patch code design via device characterization" Wencheng Wu, Edul N. Dalal (2011, Proc. SPIE 8293, Color Imaging XVII: Displaying, Processing, Hardcopy, and Applications (IS&T/SPIE Electronic Imaging), 829311) [found-abstract-only]
  https://doi.org/10.1117/12.905668
  IDEA: Patch codes encode information in printed colour patches, and their design trades decoding robustness against the number of usable code levels. The authors design optimal patch codes using an existing device characterisation (printer profile) instead of extensive print-and-measure cycles, greatly reducing the printing effort. Decoding improves markedly when the code levels are distributed in CIELAB space rather than in CMYK, and codes are optimised jointly against printing effort and device-specific noise sources.
  ACTION: Before choosing a patch count, estimate the RISO+camera noise floor (repeat identical patches across the sheet, measure the spread), then cap the number of ink levels at what is reliably separable. Space those levels evenly in a perceptual space rather than in ink duty. Include deliberate repeat patches at several sheet positions purely as a noise/uniformity estimator.

- "ISO 12642-2:2006 — Graphic technology — Input data for characterization of 4-colour process printing — Part 2: Expanded data set (ANSI CGATS IT8.7/4)" ISO/TC 130 Graphic technology (data set originally drafted by ANSI CGATS) (2006 (current edition 2015, reaffirmed 2020), International Organization for Standardization / ANSI CGATS) [found-and-read]
  https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/39371/bce636c432a24bcdb06f21b6b92afce2/ISO-12642-2-2006.pdf
  IDEA: Defines the 1617-element IT8.7/4 characterisation data set: 1588 unique CMYK ink-value combinations plus 29 deliberate duplicates retained so that visual-inspection layouts stay symmetric within tone scales and 3-colour overprint arrays. Table 1 lays out 13 structured groups — full factorials of C/M/Y at fixed black levels, single-colour scales at 21 tone values, and an explicit near-neutral group. Crucially the standard separates *data set* from *layout*: it notes the terms 'patch' and 'target' are deliberately avoided because only data values are specified, users are free to randomise the layout or fit it to available space, but two default layouts are supplied — one optimised for visual inspection and one randomised 'with the goal of uniform ink loading in each colour across the target area to minimize interaction between patch areas'.
  ACTION: Copy three things directly. (1) Separate the patch-set generator from the layout engine, and offer both a randomised layout (default, for fitting) and a grouped layout (for eyeballing). (2) Randomise specifically to equalise ink loading per region — on a RISO, drum ink starvation and the master's ink-feed profile make this even more acute than on offset. (3) Over-weight the neutral axis and per-ink tone ramps. Note that IT8.7/4 itself is CMYK-specific and so is not directly usable for arbitrary RISO drums — borrow the methodology, not the data set.

- "ECI2002 characterization target (visual and random layouts), DIN 16614" European Color Initiative working group, chaired by Günter Bestmann (Heidelberger Druckmaschinen AG), with GretagMacbeth/LOGO (2002, European Color Initiative (eci.org); standardised as DIN 16614:2004; incorporated into ISO 12642-2) [found-and-read]
  https://eci.org/doku.php_id=en_projects_eci2002.html
  IDEA: A 1485-patch CMYK characterisation target that is a strict superset of the 928-patch ISO 12642-1 / IT8.7/3 set, distributed in two variants: a structured 'visual' layout and a 'random' layout with all patch positions randomised, the latter recommended for press characterisation. A freeware utility extracts the IT8.7/3 subset from an ECI2002 measurement, so one printing yields data for both targets. The random layout is stated to reduce bias from colour variation across the page and to keep ink application even across ink zones.
  ACTION: Guarantee nesting by construction — RISO/CAM's 'quick' chart should be a literal subset of its 'full' chart, so a user who prints the full chart can also evaluate the quick fit, and a user who upgrades keeps their earlier measurements. Ship visual and random layouts of the identical patch set, defaulting to random.

- "ISO 13655:2017 — Graphic technology — Spectral measurement and colorimetric computation for graphic arts images" ISO/TC 130 Graphic technology, with ISO/TC 42 Photography (2017 (3rd edition, revising ISO 13655:2009), International Organization for Standardization) [found-and-read]
  https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/65430/dac5ec12152e4ed788d2ba4b4c234ba0/ISO-13655-2017.pdf
  IDEA: Constrains the many free choices in measuring graphic-arts specimens 'such as test charts' so results are comparable. Defines measurement conditions M0 (illuminant-A-like source, legacy), M1 (specimen illumination colorimetrically matching CIE D50), M2 (UV-cut: 400–700 nm with no substantial sub-400 nm power) and M3 (M2 plus crossed polarisers), fixes 45°:0° or 0°:45° geometry, specifies the optical properties of white backing material (critical for reproducibility, especially on thin or translucent stock), and recommends 5 nm sampling and bandpass. Its guidance also scales measurement aperture to patch size and screen ruling — coarser screens require larger apertures and hence larger patches.
  ACTION: Set a minimum patch size from the RISO's screen ruling rather than from what looks tidy, and treat that as the hard constraint the patch-count optimiser must respect — this is precisely why minimal patch sets matter for this device. Instruct users to back the sheet with a defined white backing (or a stack of the same paper) when photographing, and warn that brightened paper under a UV-rich lamp will not match results under a warm one.

- "IT8.7/5 (Idealliance TC1617) characterization target, adopted as ISO 12642-3" Idealliance Print Properties & Colour Committee; ANSI/CGATS; ISO/TC 130 (2019 (ISO designation); announcement 2023, Idealliance / ANSI CGATS / ISO 12642-3) [found-abstract-only]
  https://idealliance.org/idealliance-tc1617-designated-as-it8-7-5/
  IDEA: The successor to IT8.7/4. It removes the 29 duplicate patches that IT8.7/4 carried and spends that budget on 29 *new* grey patches, holding total patch count and chart size constant so existing measurement hardware and workflows are unaffected.   ACTION: When the chart budget is fixed, spend marginal patches on neutrals and near-neutrals rather than on saturated corners. # STRAND: camera-cal
VERDICT: the plan's accuracy concerns are justified, but the approach is viable if the capture pipeline is built correctly. The literature gives a fairly tight bracket on what is achievable.

REALISTIC ACCURACY CEILING (camera + chart):
- Laboratory asymptote for camera characterisation is ~1 Delta E (Hong/Luo/Rhodes 2001, polynomial, 12-bit, controlled).
- Best-in-class research camera with raw capture, D65 LED, leave-one-out on an SG chart: mean 1.6-3.2 Delta E (CIELUV) depending on sensor quality; linear 3x3 baseline 2.5-5.2 (Finlayson/Mackiewicz/Hurlbert, TIP 2015).
- The closest analogue to RISO/CAM - photographing printed CMYK patches with a fixed rig, raw, flat-fielded, polarised, two tungsten lamps, 928-patch training target - reached only mean Delta E00 3.07, median 2.72, max 17.8, with just 32% of 2548 test patches under 2 Delta E00 and 87% under 5 (Ashraf & Sapaico, CVCS 2018). This is the honest ceiling for a well-engineered camera pipeline.
- Phone with fixed LED illumination, fixed geometry, polariser, per-device linear calibration: CIE76 Delta E 6.45-6.60 over a full 24-patch gamut, dropping to 1.76-3.19 when training and evaluation are restricted to the colour range of interest (Cugmas & Struc, Sensors 2020).
- Phone through the normal sRGB pipeline with a chart-derived 3x3: mean Delta E 15.8 -> 7.5 after correction (Fyfe et al. 2025).
- Phone with no characterisation at all: mean Delta E* ~82 (Ciaccheri et al., Sensors 2023).
So: expect mean ~3-4 Delta E00 with a very good phone pipeline (raw + dense own-ink chart + flat-field + AR-guided geometry), mean ~6-8 with a decent one, and 15+ with a naive one. Tails matter more than means - every study reports max errors of 15-20 Delta E even when the mean is ~3.

SCANNER ALTERNATIVE (clearly better): consumer flatbed + ICC profile gives average Delta E76 1.24-1.77 with 95th percentile under 4 and max 5-10 per printer (Asman/Rippetoe/Cooper, IS&T NIP25 2009); a single generic profile across 12 printers still gave ~2.5 average / ~6.0 at the 95th. That is roughly 2-3x better than the best camera result and far more repeatable, because illumination and geometry are fixed by construction. Caveat from Lee/Bala/Sharma (SPIE EI 2006): a standard 3D RGB->Lab scanner profile does NOT generalise to arbitrary multi-colorant overprints - it gave ~6-7 average Delta E76 on unconstrained CMYK versus ~2-3 for a colorant-aware 4D characterisation. For riso's multi-ink overprints, RISO/CAM must train on riso-printed overprint patches, not a generic IT8/Q60 target.

NOISE FLOOR YOU CANNOT BEAT: LUT interpolation contributes ~1 Delta E76 and page-to-page print variation another 1.0-2.5 (Lee et al. 2006). Riso's own registration and ink-density variability will likely exceed this. There is no point engineering the capture below ~2 Delta E.

BEST PRACTICE (synthesised, each item backed above):
1. Capture raw/linear DNG, never JPEG - sRGB gamut clipping on saturated inks produces artefacts no matrix can undo (Fyfe 2025).
2. Use root-polynomial or linear correction, never plain polynomial, because phone auto-exposure breaks exposure-dependent models catastrophically (PCC degree-4 mean went 1.6 -> 7.5 -> 57 Delta E as exposure varied; RPCC stayed ~1.8) (Finlayson 2015).
3. Print RISO/CAM's own dense chart with riso inks on riso stock - 24 generic ColorChecker patches are explicitly insufficient for LUT-based correction (Ashraf 2018), and a gamut-matched custom card cut median error ~40% versus a Macbeth Classic (Nixon 2020).
4. Chart design: randomise patch order, add a same-colour border to suppress flare/integrating-cavity effects, enlarge and duplicate dark patches (Lee 2006; Asman 2009).
5. Correct spatial non-uniformity: flat-field/dark-frame (Ashraf 2018), or a first-order spatially varying correction model that lets coefficients vary across the frame (Zhang 2023). Averaging multiple captures/orientations measurably helps (Asman 2009).
6. Enforce near-perpendicular capture with on-screen AR guidance and fiducial markers - off-axis capture costs Delta E ~11 at 15 degrees rising to ~18 at 75 degrees, worst on blues (Fyfe 2025; Zhang 2023).
7. Calibrate per phone model, and expect residual per-unit variation (Nixon 2020; Jiang 2013; Vrhel & Trussell 2022).
8. Report mean, median, 95th percentile and max separately, and separately for the dark end - error tails grow far faster than means, and low-luminance samples are systematically worst (Finlayson 2015; Vrhel & Trussell 2022).

KNOWN FAILURE MODES: exposure-dependent CCM blow-up; auto white balance and ISP tone curves; sRGB gamut clipping ("shouldering"); off-axis/non-Lambertian specular reflection; illumination non-uniformity and lens vignetting; flare / integrating-cavity effects from neighbouring patches; metamerism from Luther-condition violation (irreducible, sensor-specific); dark-patch noise; partial shadows in cluttered rooms; inter-device and intra-model sensor variation; over-fitting when the training chart is sparse.

RECOMMENDATION: if RISO/CAM's goal is perceptual riso simulation rather than proofing-grade colour, a mean of ~3-5 Delta E00 from a phone is probably acceptable - but ship the scanner path as the accuracy option, gate phone captures on a quality metric (cf. Nixon's SSNR threshold), and set user expectations from the 95th percentile rather than the mean.

# STRAND: halftone-algo
Twenty papers, all verified by actually retrieving a page or PDF (several by OCR'ing scanned originals from author sites); one entry - Floyd & Steinberg 1976 - is marked referenced-secondhand because only bibliographic records, not the paper itself, are retrievable online.

The literature splits into five strands that map onto RISO/CAM's open questions.

(1) What kind of noise is riso? Ulichney's blue-noise (1988) and void-and-cluster (1993) papers define the dominant dispersed-dot paradigm and, more usefully, the measurement apparatus (radially averaged power spectrum, anisotropy, principal wavelength) you need to fingerprint real riso prints. But riso is NOT blue noise. Lau, Arce & Gallagher's green noise (Proc. IEEE 1998) is the closest published model: homogeneously distributed minority-pixel *clusters*, mid-frequency spectral peak, tunable coarseness, explicitly motivated by surviving heavy dot gain and unstable marking. Their generator - error diffusion with output-dependent feedback (a hysteresis term added to the quantizer input) - is a small, cheap change to code that already does error diffusion, with one scalar coarseness knob. He & Bouman's AM/FM halftoning (JEI 2004) and the Purdue/HP CLU-DBS line (IS&T EI 2020) reach the same texture class from the screen-design side, and the 2020 paper gives the concrete trick (large filter for initialisation, small filter for updates) plus a stability measure - effective line frequency versus tone - that a simulator should track so gradients do not change character.

(2) Joint halftoning is the highest-leverage change available. Four independent lines of work say the same thing: halftoning separations independently leaves inter-plate dot overlap to chance, and the superposition then carries low-frequency colour noise that no individual plate has. Lau et al. (IEEE TIP 2000) add an explicit cross-channel interference term to error diffusion so dot-on-dot versus dot-off-dot becomes a parameter. Damera-Venkata & Evans (IEEE TIP 2001) give the rigorous version - matrix-valued error filters, a matrix gain model, and an optimal-filter design via matrix Yule-Walker that shapes noise into low-sensitivity opponent-colour directions. Wang & Parker (JIST 2000) do the mask equivalent: design a *family* of screens jointly so each is blue noise and the union is too, with mutually exclusive dots in highlights. Agar & Allebach (IEEE TIP 2005) do it with DBS in a luminance/chrominance space and, critically, with a measurement-based printer dot-interaction model. If RISO/CAM adopts one thing from this report, it should be a per-ink-pair overlap coefficient plus jointly-generated plate screens.

(3) Overprint physics. Riso inks are pigmented and partly opaque, so the transparent-ink assumption behind classical colour halftoning is wrong - especially for fluorescents. Babaei & Hersch (IEEE TIP 2013) formalise the opposite extreme, juxtaposed (strictly non-overlapping) halftoning for opaque, metallic and iridescent inks. The practical recommendation is to treat overlap as a continuous, calibrated, per-ink-pair parameter spanning juxtaposed to dot-on-dot, rather than assuming a multiply blend.

(4) Moire and rosettes. Amidror & Hersch (JEI 2002) separate macrostructure (moire) from microstructure (rosettes) and derive when the microstructure is invariant versus varying under lateral shift between layers - which is exactly the question a riso simulator faces, since riso misregisters by design. Shen-Ge Wang & Loce (JEI 2008) give an N-colorant moire-free construction by drawing all screen frequencies from harmonics of two rosette fundamentals, which generalises past four inks where the classical screen-angle recipe does not - relevant because riso is a spot-colour process with arbitrary ink counts.

(5) Perceptual metrics. Sullivan, Ray & Miller (1991) established HVS-filtered squared error as the objective that DBS, mask design and learned halftoners all still optimise; Lieberman & Allebach (2000) show that objective carries a systematic tone bias controlled by the scale parameter (dpi x viewing distance) and that pre-correcting tone removes it. For evaluation, S-CIELAB (Zhang, Silverstein, Farrell & Wandell 1997) is the minimum correct colour-difference metric at halftone frequencies and is trivial to implement; Wang & Parker (JEI 2002) offer a more interpretable alternative, critical viewing distance, which is a natural headline number for a tool whose users care about how visible the texture is at a given print size. Jiang et al. (IEEE TIP 2023) is the most transferable recent learned-halftoning result, mostly for two loss-design lessons - an explicit anisotropy penalty is what recovers correct spectral character, and structural-similarity objectives punch holes in flat fields unless contrast-map weighted.

Gaps worth flagging: I found no peer-reviewed literature on risograph simulation specifically, and no recent (2020+) paper that revisits joint multi-colorant halftoning with modern optimisation - the joint-halftoning canon is largely 1998-2013. Ostromoukhov (SIGGRAPH 2001) and Analoui & Allebach (SPIE 1992) are marked found-abstract-only because ACM and SPIE full text are paywalled/bot-blocked; their existence, DOIs and authorship are confirmed against Semantic Scholar records.

- "Efficient Halftoning via Deep Reinforcement Learning" Haitian Jiang, Dongliang Xiong, Xiaowen Jiang, Li Ding, Liang Chen, Kai Huang (2023, IEEE Transactions on Image Processing (2023), arXiv:2304.12152 (DOI 10.1109/TIP.2023.3318937)) [found-abstract-only]

# STRAND: riso-repro
Honest headline: there is essentially **no peer-reviewed literature on risography or stencil-duplicator reproduction as such**. I searched ACM DL, IS&T, IEEE, Optica, arXiv, Springer and Wiley under many phrasings ("risograph", "risography", "stencil duplicator", "digital duplicator", "mimeograph", "thermal stencil master", "emulsion ink stencil printing") and found no journal or conference paper that models riso ink transfer, riso print appearance, or evaluates a riso simulator. What exists in the academic record about riso is sociological/practice-based (zine culture, craft studios), not physical. I found **zero** published evaluations of any riso simulation software's accuracy — every tool I located (p5.Riso, Spectrolite, Super Collage, various browser "riso simulators" and shaders, and commercial plugins) is an unvalidated look-alike effect. If RISO/CAM published even a modest calibration study against measured prints, it would be the first.

What *is* available, and what I've reported instead, falls into four strands:

**(1) Riso's own primary technical documentation — patents.** RISO Kagaku's patents are the only riso-specific sources with real numbers, and they are unusually good: US 6,011,083 gives the W/O emulsion ink chemistry and, critically, measured print density 1.21–1.26 against show-through density 0.13–0.16, plus explicit discussion of set-off and blurring mechanisms; US 5,245,932 gives the thermal master construction (1–6 µm film on an 8–13 g/m² fibre support, 400 dpi thermal head) and explains riso grain as *stochastic perforation failure and merging* at the fibre scale. That last point is the single most useful finding in this whole strand: riso grain is a master-side, spatially-correlated phenomenon, not paper noise or additive pixel noise, and simulating it that way is both more accurate and more defensible than what any existing tool does.

**(2) Stencil/screen ink-transfer physics.** Kapur, Abbott, Dolden & Gaskell (IEEE TCPMT 2013) is the rigorous model — ink is *pulled out of* the stencil by breaking liquid bridges, capillarity dominates, squeegee pressure is irrelevant to transfer. The SMTA area-ratio work supplies the measured small-aperture release curve (transfer efficiency collapses below area ratio ≈0.66), which maps directly onto riso's fine-dot dropout.

**(3) Print appearance / paper optics — this is where the field is genuinely deep,** almost all of it at IS&T, Optica and IEEE. Yang (JIST 2004) separates physical from optical dot gain and proves you need both. Inoue, Tsumura & Miyake (JIST 1997) measured paper's PSF and found it *exponential*, giving the correct blur kernel shape and scale. Sharma (IEEE TIP 2001) derives show-through from first principles with a show-through PSF and shows it must be composited in density space. Pauler et al. explain riso's low max density via Kubelka-Munk with ink penetration into a scattering sheet, including the counter-intuitive result that past an optimum, more ink *reduces* colour strength. Hébert & Hersch (JOSA A 2006) give the rigorous duplex model. Rossier, Bugnon & Hersch (IS&T CIC 2010) show dot gain depends on superposition condition — i.e. layer order matters — and that RGB-camera calibration suffices. Deshpande, Green & Pointer (Optics Express 2014) give the n-ink spot-colour overprint model with a ~2.06 ΔE00 benchmark, which is the right accuracy yardstick for RISO/CAM's ink picker.

**(4) Graphics-side print/media simulation.** Ostromoukhov & Hersch (SIGGRAPH 1999) is the standout and is under-cited in the riso-tool world: it handles halftoning with small sets of non-standard, opaque and fluorescent inks, printed side-by-side rather than overlapping — exactly riso's fluorescent-pink problem, and exactly where naive multiply-blending fails. Curtis et al. (SIGGRAPH 1997) is the canonical Kubelka-Munk layered-glaze compositing model for translucent pigment on absorbent paper. Chu & Tai (SIGGRAPH 2005) gives the GPU-practical porous-media absorption model for physically-right soft edges.

**Biggest gaps / where a simulator has to make honest choices:** nobody has published measured riso deposit-vs-coverage curves, riso misregistration statistics (drum-to-drum, sheet-feed), riso ink drying/set-off timing, or a riso ink gamut characterisation. Those will have to be measured in-house or approximated from the offset/screen literature, and the tool should be transparent about which parameters are physically grounded and which are fitted by eye. Also note two secondary sources I could not open and therefore did not report: the Rogers "Optical Dot Gain in a Halftone Print" (JIST 41, 1997) and the Hébert 2015 Color Research & Application review of halftone reflectance models — both are almost certainly worth chasing through institutional access, and the Hébert review in particular would be the fastest way to see the whole spectral-prediction landscape with reported accuracies.

- "Predicting the Behavior of Screen Printing" Nikil Kapur, Steven J. Abbott, Elisabeth D. Dolden, Philip H. Gaskell (2013, IEEE Transactions on Components, Packaging and Manufacturing Technology, 3(3), 508–515) [found-and-read]
  https://doi.org/10.1109/TCPMT.2012.2228743 (open copy read at https://www.stevenabbott.co.uk/_downloads/Predicting%20the%20Behavior%20of%20Screen%20Printing.pdf)
  IDEA: A closed-form model of how much liquid is transferred from a mesh/stencil to a substrate during screen printing. The key insight is that ink is *pulled out of* the mesh by liquid bridges as the screen snaps back, not pushed through by the squeegee: free-surface capillarity dominates, squeegee pressure plays no role in transfer. The model tracks a meniscus of varying radius between adjacent threads, splits when adjacent free surfaces meet, and predicts the residual film left on the screen; validated against on- and off-contact trials and SPTF benchmark data across many mesh counts.
  ACTION: Model per-pixel ink deposit as transfer_fraction × aperture_volume, where transfer_fraction is a function of aperture geometry and ink viscosity, and is roughly flat with drum speed. Gives a defensible reason to expose 'ink volume' as a parameter that saturates rather than scaling linearly with 'density'. Also justifies a shear-thinning viscosity curve (their Fig. 3: riso-like pasty inks drop from ~100 Pa·s at 1 s⁻¹ to ~5 Pa·s at 100 s⁻¹) if you ever simulate smearing.

- "Determining Area Ratio Rule for Type 4 and Type 5 Solder Paste" Joe Belmonte, Vatsal Shah, Rita Mohanty (Speedline Technologies); Tim Jensen, Ron Lasky (Indium Corporation) (undated conference paper in the SMTA/electronics.org technical library (content indicates mid-to-late 2000s), SMTA / electronics.org technical resource library) [found-and-read]
  https://www.electronics.org/system/files/technical_resource/E10&S33_01.pdf
  IDEA: Empirical study of stencil printing release. Transfer efficiency (deposited volume ÷ aperture volume) is governed almost entirely by 'area ratio' = aperture opening area ÷ aperture wall area. Below AR ≈ 0.66 transfer efficiency collapses and variance explodes; above it, TE approaches 100% with tight standard deviation. Finer powder lowers the usable threshold to ~0.58. Aperture shape, stencil thickness and wall finish are second-order.
  ACTION: Implement a per-dot release curve: compute an area-ratio analogue from dot radius and stencil thickness, then attenuate deposited ink with a sigmoid that goes to ~0 below the threshold and ~1 well above it, plus a variance term that *increases* as the ratio drops. This single curve reproduces dropout of small dots, ragged thin strokes, and the characteristic per-dot noise in riso mid-tones — and it is grounded in measured data rather than invented.

- "Emulsion ink for stencil printing (US 6,011,083)" Sadanao Okuda, Masato Ishikawa, Takashi Suzuki, Takahiro Wakayama, Yoshihiro Hayashi — assignee RISO Kagaku Corporation (1997 filed / 2000 granted, United States Patent (RISO Kagaku Corporation)) [found-and-read]
  https://patents.google.com/patent/US6011083A/en
  IDEA: Riso's own account of its ink. Stencil-printing ink is a water-in-oil emulsion: a continuous oil phase (50–95% non-volatile mineral/vegetable oil plus 5–50% volatile solvent, nonionic sorbitan-ester emulsifier) with an internal water phase. The invention moves the pigment into the water phase. On paper the low-surface-tension oil phase wicks in first and fast; the pigment-carrying water phase follows slowly or evaporates, so colorant stays near the surface. Reported test results: print density 1.21–1.26 with show-through density 0.13–0.16, no observable blurring or set-off; the comparative (conventional oil-phase-pigment) example gave density 0.97 with conspicuous blurring and set-off.
  ACTION: Ground the show-through channel numerically: back-side density ≈ 0.10–0.16 for a solid at front density ≈ 1.2, i.e. roughly 10–13% of front density, not a guessed alpha. Model absorption as two coupled fronts (fast oil halo + slow pigment core) so the halo is wider and lighter than the ink core — that is the physical origin of the soft fringe around riso solids. Also justifies a set-off pass (faint mirrored transfer of the previous sheet) as a real, not decorative, artifact.

- "Heat-sensitive stencil master sheet (US 5,245,932)" Assignee: RISO Kagaku Corporation (thermal stencil master construction) (1993 granted, United States Patent) [found-and-read]
  https://patents.google.com/patent/US5245932A/en
  IDEA: Describes the thermal master itself: a 1–6 µm thermoplastic (polyester/PP/polycarbonate) film laminated to a porous fibrous support of 8–13 g/m² with fibres ≤3 denier. A 400 dpi thermal head melt-punches holes in the film. The patent's whole point is a failure mode: where a fibre-bounded clearance area is larger than the head's dot pitch, the unsupported film either fails to perforate (white points inside solids) or the hole edge over-melts and adjacent perforations merge, which 'increases ink transfer' and causes 'setting-off of ink'. Fix: ≥80% of clearance areas ≤4,000 µm².
  ACTION: Generate grain from a stochastic perforation mask, not from additive noise: build a fibre-like random field (Voronoi/Worley or filtered noise at a ~50–70 µm correlation length), then per addressable dot apply P(no perforation) and P(merge with neighbour). Merged cells deposit *more* ink and bleed; unperforated cells deposit none. This gives both the white pinholes in solids and the dark blotches, correlated the way real riso is, from one mechanism.

- "A Unified Model of Optical and Physical Dot Gain in Print Color Reproduction" Li Yang (2004, Journal of Imaging Science and Technology, 48(4), 347–353) [found-and-read]
  https://library.imaging.org/admin/apis/public/api/ist/website/downloadArticle/jist/48/4/art00011
  IDEA: Separates and then unifies the two dot gains. Physical dot gain (ink actually spreading) is parameterised as Δσ = (a−1)σ₀(1−σ₀) — a single fitted parameter a per ink/substrate, symmetric and parabolic in nominal coverage. Optical dot gain (Yule–Nielsen light exchange between inked and bare regions) is ΔR_opt = p̄(1−T²)σ(1−σ), symmetric in the *actual* coverage σ, hence asymmetric when plotted against nominal σ₀. Total measured reflectance R = R_Murray-Davies − ΔR_phy − ΔR_opt. Concludes optical dot gain cannot be faked by any physical dot extension.
  ACTION: Two-stage tone pipeline: (1) geometric dilation of the halftone dot by the parabolic law with one per-ink parameter a; (2) an optical term applied after compositing that darkens midtones via σ(1−σ) weighted by paper scattering. Expose a as 'ink spread' and p̄ as 'paper scatter' — two sliders with physical meaning that together reproduce riso's characteristically crushed midtones.

- "Measuring MTF of Paper by Sinusoidal Test Pattern Projection" Shinichi Inoue, Norimichi Tsumura, Yoichi Miyake (1997, Journal of Imaging Science and Technology, 41(6), 657–661) [found-and-read]
  https://library.imaging.org/admin/apis/public/api/ist/website/downloadArticle/jist/41/6/art00016
  IDEA: Measures the modulation transfer function of paper directly by projecting sinusoidal patterns (0.375–10 cycles/mm) onto coated and uncoated stock with a modified microdensitometer, then Fourier-inverts to get the point spread function. The measured PSF of paper is well fitted by an exponential function. Applying the measured PSFs to predict halftone reflection density gave predicted densities approximately equal to measured, and estimated optical dot gain well correlated with measured optical dot gain.
  ACTION: Implement optical dot gain as a convolution with a normalised exponential kernel exp(−r/λ), λ tuned per paper (larger for uncoated/newsprint, smaller for coated), applied to the sub-surface reflectance layer only — not to the ink layer. This is a single cheap separable-ish blur that is defensible against measurement, and it is what makes simulated riso halftones stop looking like crisp digital dots.

- "Show-Through Cancellation in Scans of Duplex Printed Documents" Gaurav Sharma (2001, IEEE Transactions on Image Processing, 10(5), 736–754) [found-and-read]
  https://hajim.rochester.edu/ece/sites/gsharma/papers/showthuip01.pdf (DOI 10.1109/83.918567)
  IDEA: Derives show-through from first physical principles — light transmitted through the sheet, reflected off the backing, and transmitted back — yielding a nonlinear model that is then linearised by a log/density transform. Introduces the notion of a show-through point spread function to account for lateral spreading of light in the paper substrate, and notes that show-through has much lower dynamic range (contrast) than the front-side print. Uses it to build an adaptive filter that removes show-through given both-side scans.
  ACTION: Render the verso layer, mirror it, convolve with a show-through PSF that is markedly wider than the recto ink PSF, scale to ~10–15% of front density (matching the RISO patent's 0.13–0.16 vs 1.2), then combine in optical density space before converting to display RGB. Doing it in density space is what makes show-through correctly invisible under dark recto ink and visible only in the paper-white regions.

- "Simulation of optical properties of inkjet prints with Kubelka-Munk theory" Nils Pauler, Jerker Wågberg, Lars Eidenvall (2001, FSCN Report R-01-18, Fibre Science and Communication Network, Mid Sweden University (ISSN 1650-5387, 2001:5)) [found-and-read]
  https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:227132/FULLTEXT01.pdf
  IDEA: Computes print appearance by treating ink that has penetrated into the sheet as an ink-paper layer whose Kubelka-Munk s and k are the additive combination of ink and paper coefficients, then solving K-M. Splits 'print-through' into a show-through component and an ink-penetration component. Key results: print density saturates at a plateau once ink penetrates linearly with ink amount; print density rises when paper light scattering falls; there is an optimum ink amount beyond which colour strength *declines*; ink penetration is the main reason high colour strength is unreachable on uncoated fine paper. Halftone colours computed by combining Murray-Davies with K-M.
  ACTION: Model each ink layer as a K-M ink-paper mixture with a penetration depth parameter, and derive the ink's maximum achievable density from paper scattering rather than hardcoding it. Concretely: give each paper stock an s value, and let solid-ink appearance and the double-hit saturation ceiling fall out of it. This makes 'newsprint vs. heavier uncoated' a single physical parameter instead of a set of hand-tuned looks.

  https://doi.org/10.1364/JOSAA.23.002415
  IDEA: Models a double-sided halftone print as a diffusing substrate bounded by two inked interfaces, accounting for (a) attenuation of incident light crossing the inked interface, (b) internal reflectance and internal transmittance of the substrate including multiple internal reflections, and (c) attenuation on exit. Generalises Williams–Clapper and Clapper–Yule as special cases and extends Kubelka–Munk to predict both reflectance and transmittance of recto-verso prints.
  ACTION: If you want a high-fidelity duplex mode, implement the two-interface flux model: per-wavelength (or per-RGB-channel) transmittance of each ink layer, plus substrate internal reflectance/transmittance, with the geometric series for multiple internal reflections. Even a 3-channel reduction gives correct behaviour where heavy recto coverage *increases* apparent show-through darkening rather than masking it.

  https://doi.org/10.2352/CIC.2010.18.1.art00052
  IDEA: Extends the cellular Yule-Nielsen modified spectral Neugebauer model with an ink-spreading model. Physical dot gain of an ink is not fixed: it depends on which other inks it is superposed with, so effective surface coverage is characterised per superposition condition.   ACTION: Make ink spread a function of the substrate state, not a global constant: store a per-layer 'already inked' coverage map and modulate the dot-gain parameter for subsequent layers by it. And note the RGB-calibration result: users could photograph a printed test chart from their own riso and fit the model, without a spectrophotometer — a realistic calibration feature for RISO/CAM.

- "Characterisation of the n-colour printing process using the spot colour overprint model" Kiran Deshpande, Phil Green, Michael R. Pointer (2014, Optics Express, 22(26), 31786–31800) [found-abstract-only]
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25607147/ (DOI 10.1364/OE.22.031786)
  IDEA: Uses the spot colour overprint (SCOP) model as a generic forward characterisation model for n-colour (more than CMYK) printing, then inverts it with lookup tables to produce separations. Tested by reproducing real spot colours with a 7-colour separation on litho offset, achieving mean CIEDE2000 of 2.06 between target and printed colours.

  https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/watercolor/paper_small.pdf (DOI 10.1145/258734.258896)
  IDEA: Models a painting as an ordered set of translucent glazes, each produced by an independent shallow-water fluid simulation over a rough paper height field, with pigment advection, adsorption/desorption into the paper, and a capillary layer. The optical result of superimposed glazes is composited with a Kubelka-Munk model using per-pigment K and S, which yields glazing, edge darkening, granulation, backruns and pigment separation. Explicitly 'empirically based' rather than strictly physical — recreate the salient artist-visible effects, predictably and controllably.
  ACTION: Composite riso ink layers with per-ink Kubelka-Munk K/S coefficients over a paper reflectance, not with multiply blending — this is a small, well-specified change that fixes the muddiness of 3–4 layer overprints. Also borrow the paper height field driving pigment deposition to produce riso mottle that follows the sheet's texture rather than being uncorrelated noise.

- "MoXi: real-time ink dispersion in absorbent paper" Nelson S. H. Chu, Chiew-Lan Tai (2005, ACM Transactions on Graphics (SIGGRAPH 2005), 24(3), 504–511) [found-abstract-only]
  https://doi.org/10.1145/1073204.1073221 (record: https://researchportal.hkust.edu.hk/en/publications/moxi-real-time-ink-dispersion-in-absorbent-paper/)
  IDEA: Physically based, real-time simulation of ink spreading in absorbent paper using a lattice Boltzmann fluid model chosen specifically because it handles percolation in disordered media (paper) efficiently. Unifies spontaneous shape evolution (surface-tension-driven boundary) with porous-media flow in one framework, and couples the physics to implicit modelling plus image-based rendering for high-quality output.
  ACTION: If you want edge irregularity that is structurally right rather than a noise-modulated blur, run a small number of LBM iterations per ink layer over a paper permeability field, instead of a Gaussian dilate. Even a cheap, few-step version gives the characteristic fingered/percolating edge and correctly makes spread anisotropic where the paper field is anisotropic. Budget it as an optional high-quality mode.

- "p5.Riso — a p5.js library for generating files suitable for Risograph printing" Sam Lavigne, Tega Brain (with Crystal Chen, Paolla Bruno Dutra; NYU Integrated Digital Media) (software, ongoing (documentation retrieved 2026), Open-source software / project documentation (no peer-reviewed publication)) [found-and-read]
  https://antiboredom.github.io/p5.riso/
  IDEA: The most established piece of riso-specific prior art in creative coding. Provides channel extraction (extractRGBChannel, extractCMYKChannel, extractMappedChannels with an adjustable accuracy parameter for mapping arbitrary image colours onto a chosen riso ink set), halftoning with configurable dot shape/frequency/angle, dithering (Atkinson, Floyd–Steinberg, Bayer), and a built-in table of real riso ink colours (bright red, blue, orange, fluorescent pink, etc.), plus per-layer export ready for the printer.
  ACTION: Match its separation API surface (RGB/CMYK/mapped-channel extraction, named real ink colours, Atkinson/Floyd–Steinberg/Bayer, angle+frequency halftones, per-layer export) so RISO/CAM is a drop-in upgrade for people already working this way — then differentiate on the physics none of them have. Also worth supporting its export conventions directly so users can round-trip.
